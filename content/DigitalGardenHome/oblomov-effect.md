# Why should we worry about an "Oblomov Effect" in the age of AI?

There is a scene at the opening of Goncharov's famous 1859 novel that should feel uncomfortably familiar in 2026. Oblomov, a capable, educated Russian nobleman, lies the whole day on his sofa, wearing his famous oriental dressing gown. Not because he is sick, not because he is wicked, not even because he is simply lazy. Being in bed has become, as the narrator says bluntly, his "normal condition." Every ordinary task — writing a letter to the landlord, the administration of his estate, a reunion with old friends — presents itself as an insurmountable mountain. So he lies still, surrounded by gorgeous daydreams of a future he will never build. The novel ends with him dying on the same couch.

In clinical psychology language, the Oblomov pattern overlaps with two known failure modes of agency: pathological procrastination and abulia (a marked loss of volition in which knowing and doing decouple entirely). In cultural shorthand, "Oblomovism" came to mean a broader social lethargy that looks harmless until it has consumed a life from the inside.

A core strategic question for an AI-saturated economy is therefore: *"How do we avoid building the world's most efficient pipeline from freedom to paralysis?"* Or, conversely: *"How do we make fulfilled, creative, entrepreneurial lives more likely because AI exists?"*

AI collapses the cost of producing competence-like output at remarkable speed. That creates two simultaneous temptations: for individuals, passive consumption of algorithmically curated comfort; for institutions, delegation of tasks and judgment to AI systems that are faster but lack human perspective. Both routes lead to a high-tech version of Oblomovism, i.e. the slow surrender of agency through convenience.

The answer this article proposes is not a warning but a design brief. If we do not redesign incentives and institutions, AI will amplify the already worrying extraction of attention, data and autonomy through digital technology. If we do redesign our Societal Operating System, AI can become a general-purpose augmentation layer that increases human capability, expands the feasible set of entrepreneurial action and shifts time from drudgery toward meaningful contribution.

That redesign needs a north star. Fred Polak argued in *The Image of the Future* (1955) that civilizations rise or decline in proportion to the vividness and vitality of their images of a desirable future. A society that cannot picture a better world does not build one. Rawls (2001) gave this philosophical precision: political philosophy at its best is "realistically utopian," probing the limits of practical possibility without surrendering normative ambition. Bregman (2017) demonstrated empirically that virtually every reform once dismissed as utopian — from the abolition of child labor to women's voting rights or the welfare state — became reality within decades of being imagined. The north star is not just a destination; it is a force that shapes the journey.

The Next Economy as elaborated in this article is such a north star. Not a blueprint, but a direction that is specific enough to navigate by and alive enough to generate motion — if enough stakeholders deliberate on the pros and cons and ultimately desire its manifestation.

> ***The Next Economy is not a technology story. It is a humanistic operating-model story.***
