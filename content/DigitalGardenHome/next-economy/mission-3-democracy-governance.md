# Mission 3 — How do we upgrade democracy and governance for the third millennium?

Public-sector capacity — especially the capacity to co-create and co-lead public-private partnerships as missions — becomes a strategic priority in a fully AI-enabled economy. It becomes the difference between shared prosperity and failed states. When Rules as Code and Rulemapping make bureaucracy faster and decisions fairer, government functions like permitting, compliance, benefits administration, procurement, justice and participation become more important, not less.

## Upgrading democratic participation

Several instruments have proven themselves as means to improve decision quality and citizen engagement:

- **Liquid Democracy** — vote delegation to trusted persons on specific issues — can increase informed decision density and participation simultaneously. The German Pirate Party's implementation of Liquid Feedback from 2010 onward, Taiwan's vTaiwan digital consultation process (which successfully co-created Uber and platform economy regulation with 200,000+ participants) and multiple academic analyses of liquid delegation mechanisms demonstrate that the approach increases policy information content and citizen engagement when properly implemented (Blum & Zuber, 2016; Ford, 2002).
- **Quadratic Voting** (Lalley & Weyl, 2018) reduces extremist majority decisions by assigning progressive costs to multiple votes, giving minority preferences appropriate weight.
- **Deliberative Democracy** — randomly selected, informed citizens who assemble to share their views — has demonstrated in more than a dozen countries that informed civic self-determination produces wiser decisions than parliamentary horse-trading. Stanford's Deliberative Democracy Lab, led by James Fishkin and Alice Siu, has conducted over 150 Deliberative Polling experiments in more than 50 countries, consistently finding significant depolarization and more nuanced policy preferences after deliberation. In the landmark "America in One Room" experiments, satisfaction with democracy among Republican participants doubled from 24% to 50% after a weekend of structured deliberation (Fishkin et al., 2021). A 2024 follow-up confirmed that these attitude changes have lasting effects beyond the deliberation event itself (Fishkin et al., 2024).

A critical enabler of all these participatory approaches is the quality and balance of the information participants receive. Fishkin's method relies centrally on carefully balanced briefing materials — documents that present the strongest arguments for and against each policy option, reviewed by all parties for accuracy and fairness before distribution. Wikipedia's Neutral Point of View (NPOV) policy offers a scalable model for agreeing on factual "ground truths" that can serve as a baseline in propaganda-prone online discourse. Evolving and institutionalizing NPOV-style briefing norms across media and civic spaces is one of the most powerful structural instruments available for reducing the influence of algorithmic polarization and disinformation on collective decision-making.

Plurality (Weyl & Tang, 2023) extends this further: digital technology, properly designed, can give genuine voice to diversity rather than flattening it into majoritarian compromise or winner-takes-all scenarios. The architectural ingredients are: diverse communities, interoperating through open standards, collaborating on shared challenges without surrendering local distinctiveness. This is the political analogue of the decentralized-but-interoperable paradigm discussed for technology architecture.

## Rules as Code: making justice executable

Rules as Code is a progressive innovation project at the OECD and many countries that aims to create machine-readable codifications of laws, policies and administrative procedures in order to increase the efficiency and effectiveness of bureaucracy. Early implementations in Germany, France, the Netherlands, New Zealand, Australia and Singapore demonstrate automated compliance checking, transparent decision systems and substantial efficiency gains. Equitable tax distribution, near-real-time social support decisions and mechanisms like Tobin taxes on financial transactions to fund global common goods become practically achievable through this infrastructure (OECD, 2020).

This mission's legal and governance requirements are non-negotiable: *auditability* and human accountability for each rule application; *legibility* (citizens must be able to understand why a decision happened); *contestability* (an affordable path to challenge outcomes); and *participation* in rule creation and governance at scale. These must trump other mission objectives.
